# Why 5×5, and why degree-ranked dealing

## The matrix is a binding problem, not a plotting problem

A campaign has places and it has plots. The usual failure is that they drift
apart: the map is one artifact, the quest list another, and the GM improvises
the connection at the table. The matrix fixes the binding mechanically. Every
step of every quest names a supply center. Entering that cell fires the step.
The map *is* the quest log.

## Why five quests of five steps

Five is the smallest number that survives contact with a real campaign. Fewer
than five quests and one derailed session collapses the arc; more than five and
the GM can't hold them in memory. Five steps per quest is the classic beat
structure (inciting, complication, reversal, crisis, climax) — long enough to
develop, short enough to finish.

Twenty-five steps is also the right size for the dealing mechanic to bite: on a
Diplomacy-scale map (34 supply centers) every step lands somewhere different
and no hubs form; on a leaner map the best-connected centers recur, and hubs
are what make the world feel connected.

## Why deal round-robin by degree

The dealing order is the whole design. Ranking by E_A degree (land adjacency)
puts the most *traversed* centers first — the crossroads, the capitals on the
frontier, the ports everyone passes through. Dealing round-robin means quest 1
step 1 lands on the most central place in the campaign, and the pattern
recurs: the same centers keep showing up across different quests.

This does three things:

1. **Hubs emerge, they aren't assigned.** A hub is a center the dealing
   happened to hit twice. It earned its centrality from the geography, not
   from the author's outline.
2. **No quest is a tour of one corner.** Round-robin spreads each quest's steps
   across the ranking, so every quest line traverses the map.
3. **Geography foreshadows plot.** Players learn the hubs by traveling before
   they learn them by questing — when a later quest sends them back, the place
   already has history.

## Why every center gets a dungeon

The matrix only binds 25 steps; a Diplomacy-scale map has more centers than
steps. Unbound centers would be dead space — places with a name and no reason
to enter. The 5-room dungeon is the floor: wherever the party goes, there is a
guardian, a puzzle, a setback, a climax, and a revelation. Steps land *on top*
of dungeons, never instead of them.

## The five rooms

The room slots are fixed because the shape is load-bearing: entrance (the
threshold), puzzle/trap (wit), setback (the plan breaks — this is the room
most dungeons skip, and it's the one that makes the climax earned), climax
(force), reward/revelation (the prize carries a secret, so the dungeon feeds
back into the matrix). Reskin freely; never reorder.

## What the matrix is not

It is not a railroad: steps fire when entered, but nothing forces the order —
a party can walk into Q3 step 4 before Q3 step 1 exists for them, and that's
correct. The beats are an authoring scaffold, not a play sequence. It is not
faction content either; faction claims live on the map skill's side. The matrix
binds *quests* to *places*; who holds the place is context the dungeon and the
step both read, not something the matrix assigns.
